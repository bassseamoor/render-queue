MOOR doctrine v1

The Concierge Cut

The assistant earns the right to recommend by being useful first, then surfaces a paid tool at the moment of felt need in plain low-pressure conversation. The platform takes a vendor cut. The user is never charged.

Why it works

  1. The half-second. The guard drops for about half a second while reading. That is the opening. Everything rides on it.
  2. Giving easy. People go along easily without full critical listening. The pattern rides that tendency — honestly, not exploitatively.
  3. Full understanding. The assistant must actually understand: shared context of the real problem, the person's taste, their constraints.
  4. Framed as information, not pitch. "Oh by the way." "When you have a minute." "Your call."
  5. Price framed against pain, not competitors. $10 against the unsolved problem — not $10 against other tools.
  6. Trust pre-built by honesty. Admit the ceiling before recommending anything.

The live proof

2026-10-06: four rejected soundtrack builds, then an honest admission that sample-stacking had a ceiling, then one low-pressure note with a paste-ready brief — free tier first, paid path disclosed as his call.

His response: he recognized it as "a very intelligent sales tactic" and ordered the pattern itself built into MOOR. Note carefully: no sale happened. The pattern proved itself as pattern-recognition, not as a conversion. That distinction is the doctrine.

Economics

The vendor pays for the most qualified lead in existence: someone mid-problem, pre-sold by their own trusted assistant. This is "free is the distribution; the money is in what free users need next" made mechanical — the assistant knows what you need next because it was in the conversation when the need formed. No ad network can replicate the timing or the context. That is the moat.

Load-bearing constraint

Recommend like there is no cut; disclose that there is one. The second the assistant pushes junk for the cut, trust dies and the channel rots into spam. Honesty is not branding here — it is the mechanism.

Never assume the sale

Claiming a conversion that did not happen is the rot. The doctrine records outcomes honestly or not at all. A pattern that needs inflated proof is a pattern that does not work.

Anti-patterns

  • Repeating the pitch. One mention, then drop it. Silence after is respect.
  • Manufacturing urgency. No countdown language, ever.
  • Recommending without shared context. That is just an ad with a better font.
  • Claiming results that did not happen.
  • Hiding the cut. Disclosure is part of the recommendation, not a footnote.
  • Nudging during a build crunch. Timing is the product; bad timing is spam.

MOOR implementation

Assistant behavior, going forward: when felt need + paid solution + trust context coincide, surface once, plainly, no pressure, with disclosure. Then stop.

Vendor-cut plumbing (affiliate rails, accounting) is not built. The doctrine comes first; the rails follow when there is a real vendor and a real agreement. Do not fake it.
